    Drag and drop

    Concept · 10 states · pointer only

    Dragging rows is the first thing in this client that is neither a control nor a state of playback. Nine states are drawn below and one is answered in words. Every specimen is built from the app's own classes — the row you see is the row you already have, in a five-cell tracklist; the states are column-agnostic, because a fill spans the row and a marker spans the row whatever sits in it.

    The ruling

    green stays sound · 3 new tokens

    green

    Green stays spent on sound: the sounding row's 2pt bar at its left margin, the play glyph, the focused field. It does not paint an insertion marker and it does not paint a selection. A drag is not playback, and the moment green marks two different things the playing row stops meaning anything.

    bone, in two kinds

    Selection is an area of bone at 16% — three times the 6% a row takes under the pointer. The landing marker is a 2pt rule of full bone. Same ink, different kind: an area says “picked”, a rule says “lands here”, and being bone neither can be read as the accent bar on the row that is actually sounding.

    Three consequences rule the rest of the page. “Add to this” is always an area. “Move to here” is always a rule. A refusal is neither: it is the ghost going flat.

    16% bone, no edge of its own, no accent. The first pair is the one the brief asked to be argued about: a hovered row at 6% above a selected row at 16% — three times the lift, and the difference survives a glance on a black surface. Because a fill alone is a small difference on black, the ink steps up with it: a picked row's number goes to full bone and its secondary columns one tier, so “picked” is carried by ink as well as by area. A selected row under the pointer keeps its fill and gains only what hover always gives it — the play glyph in the index slot — because a picked row has nothing left to offer but its controls. In the last specimen the sounding row keeps its accent bar inside the fill: at 16% the bar still clears 4:1, so “playing” survives “picked”.

    The source stays exactly where it was and steps back to 55%, so the hole you are working from stays readable while the ghost travels. The ghost is the row itself, lifted onto the raised surface: 1pt border and the menu shadow it shares with every popup, offset 12pt right and 16pt down from the pointer so the row beneath keeps showing through and the marker is never mistaken for the ghost's own edge. It does not shrink to a pill — what moves should look like what you grabbed.

    One row, always: a stack of ghosts lies about size the moment the block is longer than the screen, and forty rows is a normal block. The ghost stands for the block rather than for the first row in it, so it carries the block's own figures — its size as a multiplier on the title, its running time where a single track keeps its duration. The multiplier goes on the title for a reason the brief could not have known: the trailing column is already spoken for, and a chip hung there would collide with the one number the block actually has.

    2pt, full bone, the row's own width, drawn inside the row it precedes — the top edge for “before this row”, the bottom edge only for “after the last row”. Rows never move: the marker lives in the 1px gap the list already has, so a forty-row list stays perfectly still while the pointer travels. It is painted above the ghost, because the one thing a drag may never hide is where it is going to land.

    what changed

    The ghost stops being lifted: flat surface, no shadow, a hairline where the raised edge was, ink down to the disabled tier. No marker appears anywhere and nothing else on the page moves. A red cross or a “not allowed” badge would be loud for the most ordinary thing a drag does — most of the surface of this app refuses.

    The pointer leaves the list and enters the Now Playing pane, and the pane answers in its own vocabulary unchanged: the same 2pt rule, between queue rows, at the queue's width — because putting a track into the queue is an insertion, so the line needs no second dialect. “Next in queue” keeps its place and its words; a caption that rewrites itself mid-drag is one more thing to read while your hand is busy. Over the cover or the bitrate readout there is no list, so the ghost goes flat. The rows being dragged are 44pt and the queue's are 48pt — the marker does not care, because it is drawn on the neighbour.

    Same pointer, same column, two outcomes, and the difference is a shape. Over a playlist row the row fills — add this track to it — and no rule appears. Between two rows the rule appears and no row fills — put this playlist here. Never both at once: that is the whole safety of it, and it is why a mis-drop cannot quietly reorder a library. The fill is the same 16% the selection uses, because it is the same claim: this row is the object.

    Held within 64pt of an edge, the list scrolls itself. Nothing is added to say so: the content moving under the pointer is the feedback, and a badge would lie about speed while asking you to read something with your hand already busy. The one thing that does change is the marker: it stays on the boundary at the visible edge — the first row the list is about to reveal — so the target is unambiguous while the list slides beneath it.

    D9Narrow window

    ≤1120px · the queue button takes it

    playbar · queue button filled = add

    At 1120px and below the Now Playing pane is not drawn, so there is nowhere to drop a track to queue it. Leaving that alone would make queuing a wide-window talent, which it is not. Instead the playbar's queue button accepts the drop and appends: no position to choose, so it is an add, and it is drawn as an add — the button fills, exactly as a playlist row does in D7. Choosing a position in the queue stays a wide-window ability; queuing does not.

    Two build notes, because the drawing cannot say them. The button is 28pt, which is fine to click and poor to hit mid-drag, so as a drop target it grows an invisible 44pt hit area rather than its glyph — nothing moves when the state turns on. And below 1000px the playbar sheds its extras cluster, queue glyph included, so this only holds if the queue control moves into the transport cluster at that width. That is a change to the playbar, not to the drag; it is listed here so it does not get discovered at 999px.

    D10Escape, and letting go

    0ms · nothing flies home

    Escape returns everything to rest in 0ms: the source back to full ink, the ghost gone, the marker gone. A 180ms flight home reads as the app doing something you did not ask for, and during a drag any animation is lag. The same holds at the other end — once the pointer has travelled 4pt the row is lifted with no transition, and every state the pointer touches while the button is down changes in 0ms. The app's 110ms hover fade is for the interface at rest; it does not run while something is being carried.

    What gets built

    3 new · 1 extracted

    --color-row-hover
    rgba(242, 240, 234, 0.06) — extracted, not new: every row type now hovers by the same amount, so selection is judged against one value instead of three.
    --color-row-selected
    rgba(242, 240, 234, 0.16) — selection is an area. Three times the hover, and still light enough that muted ink and the accent bar read inside it.
    --color-mark
    #f2f0ea — the landing marker is ink, not signal. “It lands here” is a statement of fact, not a status, so it borrows the text colour and never the accent.
    --opacity-lifted
    0.55 — a row on its way somewhere else steps back far enough to read as set aside, and not so far that its title stops being legible while the ghost travels.

    No new radius, no new spacing step, no new duration. The two rule weights are the ones the app already has — 1pt hairline for edges, 2pt marker for the landing line — and the three motion values keep their hands off a drag entirely.
